ROCKBACK

Summer 1962 · the first fiberglass season

A small-town evening meet under the floodlights. Cinder runway, a borrowed glass pole that bends like a fishing rod, and a crossbar that does not care how close you came.

The vault is an energy conversion machine: sprint speed goes into the bend, the bend gives it back as height. Everything here is simulated — the pole is an elastic beam, the vaulter is a jointed body fighting its own inertia, and the bar is a real object sitting on two pegs. It can rattle and stay up.

Grip height is the dial. The bag has poles from 3.80 to 4.45 — each one a little longer and a little stiffer. Grip high and there is more stick above you, but a high hold needs a full-speed run and a clean plant, or the pole never passes vertical and sets you back down where you started. Grip low and it turns over easily — and throws you straight past the bar. 4.00 is the honest one. Start there.

Two ticks are painted on the cinder for whatever hold you pick: the far one is where to press plant, the near one is where you should leave the ground. Press plant early and the tip scrapes the cinder and eats your speed; press it late and it never gets into the box at all.

The competition

Bar opens at 3.20 m and rises 20 cm each time you clear. Three attempts at each height; three misses in a row at the same height ends your night. Your score is the best height you clear, in centimetres.

Reading a vault

Plant on the mark. The painted tick on the cinder is where to press plant for your grip; the second tick is where you should leave the ground. Swing early. The pole loads while your feet are still behind you — that is the energy going in. Push late. Wait until the pole has straightened and thrown you, then extend and let go.
