PAMPERO

1928 · THE GRAIN RACE HOME · ONE WATCH OFF THE PLATE

You have the deck of the four-masted steel barque ORIFLAMME, ninety-one days out of Port Lincoln with wheat in her holds, homeward round the River Plate latitudes. Your engine is an acre of flax canvas on four sticks of steel, and every yard of it is real cloth in this machine — it bellies, it thunders, it splits along the seam that is working hardest.

Score is miles made good in the watch. More canvas is more speed. But out here lives the pampero: a line squall off the Argentine grass that arrives as a black wall and hits like a slamming door. Sail changes are not instant. Ordering the royals in sends men aloft, and in a screaming wind a fighting sail takes three times as long to gather. The glass will warn you. Shorten before it comes, not when it strikes.

THE WATCH

Six and a half minutes of your time is a full day's run of hers. A crack day's run is 280 miles. Lose a sail and it is gone for the rest of the watch. Lose a mast and the run ends where it stands — the miles you made still count.

PAUSED — CONTROLS

PRIMARY
W / ↑MAKE SAIL — order one more stage of canvas set. Crew work bottom-up: topsails, spanker, courses, topgallants, royals. Each tier takes several seconds.
S / ↓SHORTEN SAIL — order one stage in. Crew work top-down. Furling a fighting sail in a gale takes up to three times as long — this delay is the whole game.
A / ←LUFF UP — helm toward the wind. The yards are already braced to their limit, so the canvas spills, shakes and stops driving. Heel and strain fall fast. So does your speed, and every degree off course costs miles.
D / →BEAR AWAY — helm away from the wind. The yards fill, she drives and heels harder. Carried far enough she runs off before it and the apparent wind eases — the old storm dodge. It will save the rig when nothing else will, but every degree you scud off the course is a mile you do not make good.
SPACELET FLY THE SHEETS (hold) — sheets gone, sails spill and thunder. Heeling moment collapses in about a second. Every second of flogging in a strong wind chews the seams; hold it too long and you will lose the sail. Re-sheeting home takes a few seconds.

READING HER

The glass falls forty seconds ahead of a squall — the faster it drops, the harder the blow. Seam strain shows the hardest-working cloth in the ship; past the red mark the seams begin to go. Overworked seams glow on the sails themselves. The clinometer past forty degrees means her righting arm is falling away. A mast carries a limit on bending moment: exceed it for more than a moment and it goes over the side.
